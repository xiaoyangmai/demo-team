# One decision per question

Design for a child who can read short Chinese sentences but cannot hold a long procedure in mind while comparing answers.

1. Choose the exact decision point. Put the relevant facts and any already-completed steps in the question. Ask for the next action or the reason that distinguishes the choices, not an entire good-behavior routine.
2. Write three comparable alternatives at that same decision point. Each should express one main action by one clear actor. Move later steps and explanations to feedback. Avoid making the correct choice the only long, multi-clause plan: the reader should choose by the principle, not by length. Do not shorten away facts necessary to distinguish the choices.
3. Include a plausible partial solution that misses the decisive condition. Do not make every wrong option absurd or cruel. In feedback, explain the actual difference using facts in the story or question. Kindness does not require praising a false statement or claiming that an action is honest/safe/helpful when it is not.
4. Read the three choices as a set before writing the story. If one requires tracking several actions, a conditional, or a switch of actors, move prerequisites into the stem and reduce the choice to the decision being tested. Preserve the unshown application and the meaning; a shorter recall question is not a repair.

Source: [NC State, Best Practices for Creating Multiple-Choice Questions](https://teaching-resources.delta.ncsu.edu/multiplechoice/): single objectives, concise homogeneous choices, plausible distractors, no length cues. Applying this higher-education guidance to children's generated pages is a design hypothesis, not evidence of measured child learning. The independent task evaluation remains required.
